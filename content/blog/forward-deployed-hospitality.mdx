As AI gets better, building gets cheaper.

An idea that once required a designer, an engineer, a project manager, and several weeks can increasingly become a working prototype in an afternoon. The distance between describing something and making it real is collapsing.

This sounds like implementation matters less.

I think it means the quality of the input matters more.

When almost anyone can build, the advantage shifts upstream: noticing what a person actually needs, understanding why they need it, and translating that understanding into a specification clear enough for AI to execute.

I call that skill **forward deployed hospitality**.

It is the ability to get close to a human, sense what would improve their experience, turn those signals into something buildable, and stay close enough to learn whether it actually worked.

## The Bottleneck Moves

For most of the software era, execution was the expensive part.

You could understand a problem perfectly and still be unable to solve it because you lacked the time, money, or technical team. Good ideas died in the gap between intention and implementation.

AI compresses that gap. It can draft the interface, write the code, analyze the feedback, document the system, and produce ten variations before lunch.

But speed does not rescue a bad premise.

If you misunderstand the human need, AI helps you build the wrong thing faster. If your specification is vague, AI gives you a polished interpretation of your vagueness. If your taste is weak, abundance gives you more mediocre options to choose from.

The bottleneck moves from producing the work to deciding what work deserves to exist.

## Human Needs Do Not Arrive as Specifications

People rarely hand you the real requirement.

They give you symptoms.

"This dashboard is confusing."

"The room feels off."

"Onboarding takes too long."

"I wish this felt more personal."

The obvious response is to treat each sentence like an instruction. Rearrange the dashboard. Change the lighting. Remove onboarding steps. Add the customer's first name to the welcome screen.

That is responsiveness, but it is not necessarily understanding.

The confused dashboard user may really be afraid of making an irreversible decision. The room may feel off because nobody knows where to stand when they arrive. Onboarding may feel long because the user cannot see progress or value. Personalization may have nothing to do with a name and everything to do with remembering context.

The stated request is a clue. The need lives underneath it.

Finding that need requires attention, curiosity, and judgment. You have to notice pauses, repeated complaints, workarounds, body language, emotional stakes, and the difference between what people say and what they consistently do.

Then you have to make a bet about what all those signals mean.

## Hospitality Is a Specification Skill

Hospitality is often reduced to warmth: smile, remember a name, offer a drink, be nice.

Warmth matters, but great hospitality is more precise than that.

It notices that someone is cold before offering a blanket. It knows whether a surprise will create delight or anxiety. It understands that the solo diner does not merely need a table; they need to feel that they are not an inconvenience. It tells a guest what shoes to wear because the beautiful route includes soft grass.

Care becomes real through details.

That makes hospitality a form of requirements gathering. A great host is constantly converting human signals into decisions about timing, environment, information, and action.

AI is becoming excellent in the middle of this chain. Give it a clear outcome and enough context, and it can help translate a specification into shipped behavior.

Humans still have disproportionate leverage at the edges: perceiving the original signal and judging the response.

## Why "Forward Deployed"?

Forward deployed engineers work close to the customer and the actual environment where software has to perform. They do not receive a pristine requirements document and disappear to build. They enter the messy workflow, understand the constraints, ship something narrow, watch it operate, and feed what they learn back into the product.

Hospitality should work the same way.

You cannot fully understand a human experience from a conference room. You have to get close to the moment.

Watch someone use the product. Sit in the waiting room. Walk the arrival path. Listen to the support call. Try to find the bathroom without asking. Notice when a person hesitates, apologizes, abandons the task, or invents a workaround.

Then deploy care directly into that environment.

Not as a grand gesture. As a better default, a clearer instruction, a graceful recovery, a remembered preference, or one well-timed human intervention.

The work is forward deployed because the truth is in the field.

## The Premium Skills

If implementation becomes abundant, a different set of skills becomes scarce.

**Observation.** Can you see friction that people have normalized? Can you notice the detail everyone experiences but nobody reports?

**Listening.** Can you hear the need behind the request without rushing to prove that you have an answer?

**Context.** Can you rapidly understand the culture, incentives, language, and emotional stakes surrounding the problem?

**Synthesis.** Can you combine scattered signals into a coherent explanation of what is actually happening?

**Taste.** Can you distinguish a technically correct result from one that feels obvious, calm, and deeply considered?

**Specification.** Can you express an insight with enough clarity that another human or an AI system can build from it?

**Judgment.** Can you decide which need deserves attention, which solution is proportionate, and where automation should stop?

**Follow-through.** Can you stay after launch, observe the consequences, and revise your theory when reality disagrees?

These are not soft skills orbiting the real work. They determine whether the real work creates value.

## Care Without Surveillance

There is an obvious danger in combining AI, personal context, and hospitality.

The tools that help us remember can also help us manipulate. The desire to anticipate needs can become an excuse to collect everything. Personalization can become a machine for extracting more attention or money while pretending to care.

The standard should be simple: collect only what you can use to improve the person's experience, and use it in a way they would be comfortable understanding.

Good hospitality increases agency. It removes friction, clarifies choices, preserves dignity, and makes it easier for someone to get what they came for.

Bad hospitality uses intimacy as leverage.

AI makes both approaches easier. Judgment determines which one we deploy.

## An Operating Loop

Forward deployed hospitality can be practiced as a loop:

1. **Get close.** Observe the experience where it actually happens.
2. **Collect signals.** Listen for requests, but also notice hesitation, repetition, workarounds, emotion, and context.
3. **Name the need.** Ask what outcome or feeling sits beneath the visible symptom.
4. **Write the spec.** Translate that need into a concrete change, including boundaries and a definition of success.
5. **Use AI to build.** Let machines compress the implementation work where they are useful.
6. **Deploy narrowly.** Put the change into the real environment without betting the entire experience on an untested theory.
7. **Sense the response.** Watch what improves, what breaks, and what people do next.
8. **Refine the system.** Turn what worked into a repeatable capability without removing the human judgment that made it work.

This is the product version of [Service by the Senses](/blog/service-by-the-senses): observe more inputs, connect them to human context, and turn care into operations.

## The Human Advantage

The future will contain an extraordinary amount of competent output.

More software. More content. More interfaces. More automations. More answers generated immediately and delivered at nearly zero marginal cost.

That abundance makes specificity more valuable.

Who is this for? What are they trying to do? What are they afraid of? What have they stopped noticing because the friction is so familiar? What would make the experience feel not merely functional, but considered?

The winners will not only be the people who know how to prompt AI. They will be the people who know what to ask it to make—and can explain why that thing should exist.

Implementation becomes abundant.

Care stays specific.

The premium belongs to those who can translate between the two.
