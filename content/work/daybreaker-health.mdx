Daybreaker Health is my current build: a longevity operating system for people who want more good years, not just more years. The product combines diagnostics, lab interpretation, behavior design, coaching operations, and AI-assisted protocol generation into a member experience that can be delivered repeatedly without becoming generic.

## Problem

Personalized health planning is still too fragmented. A motivated person can collect labs, wearables, nutrition logs, body composition scans, genetic data, supplement plans, and fitness assessments, but the work of turning that pile into an executable protocol is still slow, manual, and inconsistent.

The operational problem is the same one I have seen across healthcare and fitness: the valuable work happens in a messy middle layer. Data comes in from different places. A practitioner has to reason across it. A member needs a plan simple enough to follow. The business needs enough structure that the service can scale without losing judgment.

## What I Built

I built Daybreaker around a one-year member journey:

- Assessment and protocol design: lab work, genetics, functional diagnostics, goals, constraints, and baseline risk profile.
- Protocol implementation: sleep, nutrition, movement, environment, therapies, aesthetics, and prescription-adjacent workflows where appropriate.
- Retesting and optimization: biomarker retesting, protocol revision, and progress tracking.
- Autonomy and maintenance: a handoff model that helps members keep executing after the highest-touch phase.

The core product work included:

- A member dashboard for labs, diagnostics, protocol status, and follow-up actions.
- A biomarker database with optimal ranges, not just standard lab reference ranges.
- Program logic for sleep, nutrition, movement, environment, recovery therapies, aesthetics, and prescription workflows.
- AI-assisted analysis flows that turn dense health data into draft insights, questions, and protocol recommendations for human review.
- Internal operating systems for onboarding, documentation, newsletter/content production, and member communication.

## Product Principles

Daybreaker is designed around a few constraints:

- Data should make the next action clearer. A dashboard that makes someone feel informed but does not change behavior is theater.
- Protocols need enough specificity to be useful and enough flexibility to survive real life.
- AI should compress research, drafting, and pattern recognition, but a human should own the judgment and clinical boundary.
- The product should feel high-trust: direct, specific, and disciplined, without drifting into hype.

## Execution

The early build focused on proving the service loop before over-automating it. That meant doing the manual work first: mapping every member touchpoint, building the intake and diagnostic structure, writing protocol templates, identifying which biomarkers mattered most, and turning repeated judgment calls into reusable product rules.

From there, I moved pieces into software:

- Lab and diagnostic tracking moved from static documents into structured data models.
- Protocol sections became reusable modules, each with metrics, goals, and review cadence.
- AI prompts were used to draft summaries, identify missing information, and speed up practitioner review.
- Content and newsletter systems became part of the product surface, because trust is built before a person ever books a call.

## Impact

Daybreaker turned a broad longevity concept into a repeatable service model: intake, assessment, protocol design, coaching, retesting, and long-term maintenance. It gave the business a clearer operating system and gave members a more concrete path from health data to daily action.

The product is still evolving, but the central thesis has stayed stable: personalized healthcare gets better when the underlying system is specific enough to measure, structured enough to scale, and human enough to be trusted.

## Lessons

Healthcare products have to earn trust twice. They need technical credibility, and they need operational reliability. If the dashboard is beautiful but the follow-up is inconsistent, the experience fails. If the protocol is scientifically interesting but impossible to follow, the outcome fails.

The work is to turn a complex body of information into something a person can actually do on a Monday morning.
